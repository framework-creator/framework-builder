# Business Strategy Framework

*This framework was generated using the Framework Builder methodology. It demonstrates the five-layer DNA architecture applied to business strategy for small and mid-size organizations.*

Page: https://whereframeworks.com/frameworks/business-strategy
Guide: https://howtoframework.com/business-strategy-framework

---

## Layer 1: Principles Foundation

**Principle 1: Strategy is a set of choices, including what you refuse.** A goal like "grow revenue by 30%" is a destination. Strategy tells you which paths to take, which to reject and how to decide at a fork.

**Principle 2: Depth beats breadth when you are the smaller player.** Serving fewer segments at a higher level of expertise beats competing as a generalist on price.

**Principle 3: Every engagement should set up the next one.** Scope work to show visible results quickly, so each client has a reason to come back.

**Principle 4: Set a delivery limit and honor it.** Pick the point where quality degrades, such as the number of outside contributors per project, and decline work beyond it.

**Principle 5: Structure beats volume.** Proposals are judged against the strategy, not against who pitched them most passionately.

## Layer 2: Systematic Approach

**Step 1: Assess the situation.** Write down your market position (leader, challenger, niche), your resource limits and how many service lines you run. These three together point to a strategy type.

**Step 2: Pick the strategy type.** A challenger with limited resources and one service line points to focused differentiation. A leader points to defense and scale. Write down why.

**Step 3: Choose where to compete.** Select a small number of segments based on existing expertise and where your client base is already concentrated.

**Step 4: Allocate effort by a fixed rule.** One workable split: 70% on core segments, 20% on adjacent opportunities referred by existing clients, 10% on exploring a possible third segment.

**Step 5: Name the capabilities you must build.** List what the strategy requires that you do not have today. That gap is where investment decisions live.

## Layer 3: Force Multipliers

**Multiplier 1: The strategy filter.** Three yes-or-no questions every new opportunity must pass before it gets a proposal. Example: is it in a core segment, can we deliver a measurable result within 90 days, does it need two or fewer outside contributors? Any no goes to the "not now" list.

**Multiplier 2: The kill list.** Write the work you will not do, with the reason beside each item. Examples: fixed-bid projects over your risk limit, or work that only supplies staff.

**Multiplier 3: Make leaders use it first.** Founders run their own referrals through the filter in public for the first three months.

**Multiplier 4: Put the filter in the proposal template.** Then anyone can say no, with the card in hand, without escalating.

**Multiplier 5: Name the capability gap with an owner.** For each capability the strategy needs and you lack, assign a person and a date. Otherwise the gap stays a wish.

## Layer 4: Success Metrics

**Positive indicators:**
- Revenue concentration in core segments rises toward the target (for example, above 75% in 18 months)
- Repeat engagement rate above 60% within 12 months
- Every proposal passed the filter, with documented exceptions
- Engagement margin holds at a premium level
- At least three qualified opportunities in each core segment at all times

**Failure signals:**
- The filter is overridden often, and the overrides are not logged
- Revenue is growing in segments you did not choose
- The strategy is only reviewed once a year
- Different people explain the strategy differently
- A major client is lost and no one revisits the assumptions

## Layer 5: Implementation Guidance

**Convert the strategy into a one-page card in quarter one.** Filter, kill list, allocation rule. Add content and events by segment as the first quarterly objectives.

**Hold a monthly strategy review with three agenda items.** Filter exceptions from the month, the metrics, and whether any assumption about your chosen segments needs updating.

**Define reassessment triggers.** Losing a major client, a competitor entering your space or a technology shift forces a review before the calendar does.

**Common mistake: building around current capabilities only.** That is an inventory, not a strategy.

**Edge case: a lucrative request outside your segments.** Put it on the "not now" list with a polite referral. If it keeps coming, treat it as evidence for a review, not an exception.

**Edge case: a partner brings in off-strategy work.** Same filter, same rules. That is the point of using it publicly.

**Common mistake: confusing goals with strategy.** "Grow revenue by 30%" tells you where to aim. It does not tell you which path to take or which to reject. Add the choices.

**Common mistake: trying to serve everyone.** "Small business and mid-market and enterprise" is the absence of a strategy. It dilutes positioning, scatters resources and loses to rivals who chose one thing and committed.

**Common mistake: setting strategy once a year.** An annual cycle assumes your assumptions hold for twelve months. Write the reassessment triggers into the framework, and review on the first Monday of each month.

**Common mistake: letting the loudest voice drive direction.** Without a framework, the most confident person wins. Evaluate every proposal against the filter, including your own.
