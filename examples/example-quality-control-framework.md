# Quality Control Framework

*This framework was generated using the Framework Builder methodology. It demonstrates the five-layer DNA architecture applied to quality control for creative and technical work.*

Page: https://whereframeworks.com/frameworks/quality-control
Guide: https://howtoframework.com/quality-control-framework

---

## Layer 1: Principles Foundation

**Principle 1: Quality belongs to the person making the work.** The quality function verifies the system. It does not compensate for people who throw work over the wall.

**Principle 2: Nothing ships without a second set of eyes.** Review is not optional, whatever the seniority or deadline.

**Principle 3: Ask which process allowed it, not who did it.** Blame teaches people to hide problems, and hidden problems turn into serious ones.

**Principle 4: "Good" is written down before work starts.** If the team cannot describe it, they are guessing, and quality becomes a matter of opinion.

**Principle 5: Prevention costs less than rework, and rework costs less than losing customers.** Spend the money early.

## Layer 2: Systematic Approach

**Step 1: Identify the critical control points.** Where can a defect be introduced or caught? A typical set: requirements sign-off, peer review, an integration or staging check and a pre-launch checklist.

**Step 2: Route by size of work.** Small maintenance tasks take a lightweight path with a single reviewer. New builds take the full path with structured review, staging verification and a client preview.

**Step 3: Write pass/fail criteria for each control point.** Put them in templates so reviewers are not guessing what to check.

**Step 4: Write a definition of done per work type.** Makers check their own work against it before asking for review.

**Step 5: Investigate each escape.** When a defect reaches a customer, hold a short root-cause review, trace it to the process gap and update the checklist or template.

## Layer 3: Force Multipliers

**Multiplier 1: Automate the checkable.** Linting and tests on every submission catch formatting errors and basic logic bugs before a human spends time on them. This is error-proofing.

**Multiplier 2: Definition-of-done documents.** One per project type, short enough to use.

**Multiplier 3: A fifteen-minute root-cause review for every production defect.** The output is a process change, not a name.

**Multiplier 4: Fix the process, not the inspection.** Before adding a review step, ask what created the defect. A fixed process needs fewer inspections.

## Layer 4: Success Metrics

**Positive indicators:**
- Production defects per project in the first 30 days stay under your target (for example, under three)
- Review rejection rate settles at a level that shows clear standards
- Average rework hours per project fall
- Client-reported issues per quarter fall

**Failure signals:**
- Rejection rates are high because standards are unclear
- Defects fall while rework rises (prevention is working but standards may be too rigid)
- Reviews are skipped under deadline
- Every defect ends with a person being named
- New inspection steps keep appearing without a change in defect type

## Layer 5: Implementation Guidance

**Roll out in three phases over about 90 days.** Weeks 1 to 2: write definitions of done for the three most common project types and automate the checks. Weeks 3 to 6: formalize the review template and start tracking the four metrics. Weeks 7 to 12: begin root-cause reviews and hold the first monthly quality review against baseline data.

**Review trends every six months.** Read all four metrics together, not one at a time.

**Common mistake: treating quality as overhead.** Calculate what rework and lost clients cost before cutting prevention.

**Edge case: a deadline collides with review.** Shorten the scope of work, not the review. If the review must be reduced, record the exception and the owner of the risk.

**Edge case: a senior person resists review.** Apply the rule to everyone, and let the checklist, not the reviewer, carry the standard.
